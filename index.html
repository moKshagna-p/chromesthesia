How sound becomes vision
  • Lightness follows pitch height: low notes dark, high notes light (Ward et al. 2006, synaesthete data).
  • Colourfulness peaks around B3. Piano and strings look more vivid than pure tones (Ward 2006).
  • Hue follows the note: C red, D yellow, E yellow-green, F green, G cyan-blue, A blue-violet, B violet. Saturation drops from C to B (Itoh et al. 2017).
  • Each note is a soft cloud of light that blooms with the sound, fades with it, and leaves its colour lingering in the field for a few seconds, so a song builds up its own wash of colour. Higher notes sit higher and are smaller; louder notes are bigger (Chiou et al. 2013).
  • Shape follows timbre. Soft sounds make wide, diffuse clouds; bright, harsh sounds (brass-like, buzzy synths, cymbals) make denser, more contrasting, splintered forms (Adeli et al. 2014, replicated 2021). Nothing has hard edges: synaesthetes describe and draw blurred light.
  • Texture: rough sounds (beating, dissonance, muddy low chords) look grainy with a ragged edge and flicker; clean tones look smooth. Listeners treat "rough" and "sharp" as different shapes, and synaesthetes describe textures like "granulated", soft centres and fuzzy edges.
  • Gliding pitch drags a soft trail through the field, like a sparkler (a synaesthete's description in Ward 2006).
  • Left/right follows the stereo position of the sound. A mic is mono, so everything sits near the centre.
  • Chords show one colour per note. Overlapping objects add up like light.
  • Overall tint follows tempo and key: fast/major music turns the whole field light, warm and vivid; slow/minor music turns it dark, blue and muted (Palmer et al. 2013).
